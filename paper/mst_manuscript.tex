\begin{abstract}
A camera network that reports how far a target moved must spend a finite
processing budget. Every camera frame that is transferred, decoded, detected
and triangulated costs the same, so a fixed budget can buy more simultaneous
views at each time instant, or more instants. The two choices act on opposite
biases of one estimate: more instants reduce the corner cutting that makes a
sparsely sampled polyline too short, while more views reduce the point noise
that makes a densely sampled one too long. That trade-off is established for
single-sensor telemetry, where the sampling rate is fixed by the device, but
the allocation question it raises for a camera network is not. We characterise
the two-dimensional error surface on a seven-camera rig with an independent
optical tracking reference, under a protocol registered in advance whose
predictions were sealed before the reference was read. Three views and seven
views give path-length errors that cannot be distinguished at any sampling
density tested, so the budget should buy instants: the same accuracy is
reached with forty-three percent of the frames. We then explain why the extra
views are wasted. A geometric model with independent pixel noise predicts a
large gain from seven views, and the measured ratio shows none, because the
reconstruction errors are largely shared across views and a path length is
invariant to a common translation of its points. Two further results bound
what remains to be tuned, and a single-camera chain is shown to have the
opposite optimum.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A recurring measurement task is to report how far a target moved over a time
window: the arc length of its trajectory, rather than the straight-line
displacement between the endpoints. When the trajectory is observed only at
discrete instants, the estimate is biased in two directions at once.
Connecting sparse samples by straight lines cuts every corner and
underestimates the arc. Connecting dense samples accumulates the per-sample
position noise, and in the limit of infinitely dense sampling the polyline
length diverges.

This trade-off is established. Palmer [1] simulated it for fishing-vessel
tracks and noted that the overestimation at high polling rates is inversely
proportional to vessel speed. Ranacher \textit{et al} gave a closed form for
the noise-driven overestimate [2] and devoted a separate study to locating the
balance point for floating-car data [3]. Noonan \textit{et al} [4] established
the full picture for animal telemetry, proved the divergence result, named the
balance point the `Goldilocks sampling frequency', argued that it is
impractical to hit deliberately, and published a corrective estimator.
Rowcliffe \textit{et al} [5] and Schoombie \textit{et al} [6] quantified the
two limbs empirically. We claim none of this as new and we build on it
directly.

It is also known that adding cameras to a markerless motion-capture rig helps
less than one might expect. Mulla \textit{et al} [7] found that two cameras
can match four for markerless hand tracking, and Walker \textit{et al} [8]
found that eighteen, twelve and six cameras change biomechanical kinematics by
only a few degrees. We report the existence of saturation as confirmation of
their finding, not as a discovery.

What neither literature contains is the allocation question that these two
facts jointly create. A telemetry collar has one sensor and a fixed duty
cycle, so the sampling interval is its only lever, and the practical response
to the bias trade-off is post-hoc correction. A camera rig has two levers, and
they compete for the same budget. If the cost that matters is the number of
camera frames that must be transferred, decoded, detected and triangulated,
then a budget of $B$ frame reads per window satisfies
\begin{equation}
B = k\,m,
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the number of simultaneous views used at each instant and $m$ is
the number of instants. The design question is where to spend. The saturation
studies [7, 8] vary the camera count at a fixed frame rate, and so measure
only one edge of that surface.

This paper contributes the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{A budget-equalised allocation result.} Under a fixed frame
budget the budget should buy instants, down to the point where view count
saturates. On our rig three views match seven at every sampling density
tested, so the same measurement is obtained with 43\% of the processed frames;
at equal cost, three views at nineteen instants give 14.9\,mm against
27.9\,mm for seven views at sixteen instants.

\item \textit{A mechanism for the saturation, and evidence that extra views
can be harmful.} An independent-pixel-noise model predicts a 41\% gain from
seven views over three; the realised ratio is 1.08 on average. The
reconstruction errors are largely common-mode, with a median cosine of 0.87
between the three-view and seven-view error vectors, so the extra views have
little independent noise to average. On all five development records the
seven-view solution improves when a single camera is removed, by 4\% to 45\%.

\item \textit{Two bounds on further tuning.} Non-uniform placement of the
instants gains only 8\% to 25\% even with oracle access to the reference, and
the deployable closed-form rule is worse than uniform sampling. Separately,
the apparent dense-sampling degradation in our data is gross-outlier exposure
rather than noise accumulation; the median and the ninetieth-percentile error
fall monotonically, and a consistency gate, not a different allocation, is the
appropriate response.
\end{enumerate}

We follow the vision-based measurement framing of Shirmohammadi and Ferrero
[9]: the camera rig is the instrument, and the object of study is the
measurand and its uncertainty, not the performance of an algorithm.
Section~\ref{sec:opposite} reports the same allocation question for a
single-camera chained-registration system, where the optimum sits at the
opposite end, which is why we present the allocation as a quantity to be
measured per instrument rather than as a constant.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sampling-density bias in distance travelled}
The two-sided bias, the interior optimum, and its dependence on the ratio of
motion per interval to measurement noise are established in [1--4]. Noonan
\textit{et al} [4] give a practical sampling window bounded below by the ratio
of error variance to velocity variance and above by the velocity
autocorrelation time, prove that no estimator recovers distance when the
sampling interval exceeds three times that autocorrelation time, and provide a
continuous-time speed and distance estimator. Codling and Hill [10] give
analytic expressions for how apparent step length scales with sampling rate in
a correlated random walk; Jerde and Visscher [11] and Hurford [12] document
the noise-driven corruption of step lengths and turn angles. The fractal and
tortuosity literature [13, 14] is the conceptual antecedent. All of this work
treats a single sensor with a fixed sampling rate.

\subsection{Camera count in multi-view measurement}
Triangulation uncertainty as a function of camera count, baseline and noise is
characterised in [15], and diminishing returns are expected. Markerless
motion-capture studies report saturation empirically [7, 8, 16]. Leizea
\textit{et al} [17] build an uncertainty budget for a multi-camera system and
test the premise that more viewpoints lower uncertainty. These analyses hold
the temporal sampling fixed and vary only the view count, so they do not
address a shared budget.

\subsection{Network design and negative results in measurement}
Shen \textit{et al} [18] derive network-configuration guidance from an error
characterisation of an industrial photogrammetric system, which is the closest
methodological precedent to what we do, but for a static network and a static
measurand. Nasiri \textit{et al} [19] show that a textbook optimal
triangulation method is not optimal, and Das \textit{et al} [20] show that a
widely used agreement statistic misrepresents markerless versus marker-based
comparison; our sections~\ref{sec:nonuniform} and \ref{sec:outliers} are of
that kind.

\subsection{Positioning}
Our contribution is the budget axis of equation~\eqref{eq:budget}, the
mechanism behind the saturation, and the two bounds. The bias mechanism
belongs to [1--4]; the existence of saturation belongs to [7, 8].

\section{Measurand, measurement chain and cost model}
\label{sec:chain}

\subsection{Measurand}
The arc length of the target's trajectory between two specified instants, in
metres. We report it separately from the endpoint displacement, which is a
different quantity and behaves differently under sampling.

\subsection{Chain}
At each selected instant the target's image position in each selected camera
is triangulated by the direct linear transform into a three-dimensional point.
The sequence of positions is converted to a length by one of three estimators:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Polygon}: the sum of the segment lengths.
\item \textit{Limited arc}: a segment-wise Hermite interpolation with a
limiter that removes backward longitudinal overshoot while retaining
transverse curvature, integrated by 32-point Gauss--Legendre quadrature.
\item \textit{State mean}: the length of the conditional mean path of a
Wiener-velocity state-space model fitted to the selected positions, with the
acceleration spectral density chosen by innovation likelihood [21]. This is
the strongest of the three at realistic budgets and is the primary estimator
throughout.
\end{itemize}

A fourth estimator integrates the conditional expected speed. By Fubini's
theorem the integral of the expected speed equals the expectation of the
integrated speed, so under our state model this is exactly the estimator of
[4]; section~\ref{sec:ctsd} reports it with the observation noise calibrated
as [4] requires.

\subsection{Cost model}
One camera frame read at one instant is one unit, so a window costs $B=k\,m$.
Frames in which the detector returns nothing are still charged. This counts
transfer, decoding, detection and triangulation, and deliberately does not
count acquisition: a rig that records seven cameras and processes three has
already paid for the sensors. The model therefore answers how much of a
recorded multi-camera stream must be processed, not how many cameras to buy.

\subsection{Uncertainty budget}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
For the recommended configuration ($k=3$, $m=33$, 60 confirmation windows,
mean measurand 6.625\,m) the budget is evaluated top-down, in the vocabulary
of the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement and of the
International Vocabulary of Metrology, and is given in
table~\ref{tab:uncertainty}. The result is stated as: path length $=$ measured
value $+\,8.2$\,mm, a measurement bias that we determine but do not apply,
with an expanded uncertainty $U = 14.9$\,mm at coverage factor $k_{c}=2$ and
approximately 95\% coverage, that is 0.22\% of the measurand.

The reconstruction error is deliberately not a separate term: it is one of the
effects that varies between windows and is therefore already inside $u_{A}$.
We instead use it as a consistency check, and the check is informative. The
measured per-point position error is 5.84\,mm root mean square; propagated
incoherently over the 32 segments it would predict a dispersion of 46.8\,mm,
against the 7.43\,mm observed, an over-prediction of 6.3 times. The reason is
that a path length is invariant to a common translation of its points, so the
common-mode component identified in section~\ref{sec:mechanism}, which
dominates the position error, largely cancels in the length. This is why the
length is a more robust measurand than position, and it is the same fact that
makes extra views nearly worthless here: additional views can only reduce the
independent component, and the independent component is the only one that
survives into the length.

Coverage is asserted from a normal approximation rather than verified by a
coverage study; windows within a record share a rig, a calibration and a
session, so the effective degrees of freedom are below 59; and
$b_{\mathrm{samp}}$ is configuration-specific, growing rapidly as $m$ falls
below 25. Where we compare configurations we use paired differences on the
windows that both arms completed, with bootstrap intervals.

\subsection{Failure policy}
An instant at which fewer than two views yield a detection is dropped from the
polyline. If either endpoint instant is lost, the window is a failure.
Failures are counted and never removed from denominators.

\section{Data and pre-registered protocol}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Primary rig}
Seven hardware-synchronised cameras at 50\,Hz observe a single passive marker
carried through free-space motion, and an optical tracking system records the
marker at 200\,Hz as an independent reference [22]. We use the publisher's
released per-camera two-dimensional centroids and intrinsics, with extrinsics
fitted once on a held-out record. Each record gives 15 non-overlapping windows
of 3.98\,s, that is 200 camera frames. Development used records 19 to 23,
giving 75 windows. Confirmation used records 15 to 18, giving 60 windows,
previously touched only by a per-frame triangulation quality check and never
by any path, sampling or estimator design.

\subsection{Secondary chain, opposite regime}
Three sequences from a public colour-and-depth benchmark [23] with a single
moving camera and an optical tracking reference for the camera pose. Positions
come from chained pairwise rigid registrations, not independent triangulation.
Development used the 15 windows at offsets 1 to 17\,s used by earlier work;
confirmation used the 14 valid windows at offsets of at least 21\,s, never
previously touched.

\subsection{Pre-registration}
For each confirmation the protocol, candidate list, camera-subset rule, gates
and source-file hashes were frozen before any confirmation record was read,
and all predictions were computed and sealed by a cryptographic hash before
the reference was loaded. Sealing timestamps and hashes are in the released
artefacts.

\subsection{Reference caveat}
The reference length is the polyline of the 200\,Hz trace between the window
endpoints, itself a sampled estimate. Decimating it by two changes the
reference length by a few millimetres, the scale of the smallest differences
we report.

Three views and seven views are indistinguishable at every density, and the
difference has no consistent sign. A paired comparison confirms this. Taking
for each of the 35 triples the mean absolute error over the windows that it
and the seven-view configuration both completed, the median difference for
$m \geq 19$ is $-0.20$ to $-0.38$\,mm in favour of three views, 19 to 21 of

zero at every $m \geq 19$. At $m=16$ the comparison is dominated by one window
in which a view dropped out and the triple degenerated to two views, producing
a 3.1\,m error; that window is the subject of section~\ref{sec:outliers}.

Two views are consistently worse and, more importantly, they fail: across the
21 pairs, 250 window-arms lost an endpoint to a missing detection, against
zero for every triple. Three views is the smallest subset that is both
accurate and reliable on this rig.

The allocation consequence follows. At $m=33$ the three-view configuration
costs 99 frames against 231, for the same accuracy: the same measurement at
43\% of the processed frames. At equal cost, three views at 19 instants, that
is 57 frames, give 14.9\,mm while seven views at 16 instants, that is 112
frames, give 27.9\,mm, so halving the cost also roughly halves the error.

\subsubsection{The geometric prediction.}
Under independent isotropic pixel noise the triangulation covariance is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}) = \sigma_{\mathrm{px}}^{2}
\left( \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbf{J}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{i} \right)^{-1},
\label{eq:cov}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{J}_{i}$ is the $2\times3$ projection Jacobian of camera $i$ at
the point $\mathbf{X}$. Evaluated along the reference trajectory, this model
predicts a seven-view to three-view error ratio of 0.583 to 0.593 across the
five records, that is, seven views should be about 41\% more accurate.

\subsubsection{The realised ratio.}
The realised ratio averages 1.08, and on two records the seven-view solution
is worse than the three-view one.

\subsubsection{The reason is common-mode error.}
The median cosine between the three-view and seven-view error vectors is 0.866
to 0.881 on every record. The two reconstructions err in nearly the same
direction, so roughly seven eighths of the error is a shared component that
averaging over more views cannot remove. This is the same structure that
Ranacher \textit{et al} [2] carry as a correlation factor in their closed
form: averaging removes the uncorrelated part only. Plausible physical sources
here are extrinsic-calibration residuals, common to every reconstruction that
uses those cameras, and a marker-centroid bias that depends on the target's
orientation and on motion blur and therefore moves together in all views at
once. We do not separate them.

\subsubsection{Extra views can be harmful.}
On all five records the seven-view solution improves when one camera is
removed, by 3.7\% to 44.8\%. A single degraded view is not rejected by an
unweighted least-squares solution, and on records 21 and 23 it dominates. This
is why seven views are not merely no better than three but sometimes worse,
and it is a practical argument for view selection or robust weighting rather
than for using every available camera.

\subsubsection{Why this matters more for a length than for a position.}
The measurand compounds the effect. A path length is invariant to a common
translation of its points, so the common-mode component largely cancels in the
length even though it dominates the position error;
section~\ref{sec:uncertainty} shows this quantitatively, with incoherent
propagation of the per-point error over-predicting the observed dispersion by
a factor of 6.3. The two facts together leave extra views very little to do.

\subsubsection{Consequence for the design guideline.}
A geometry-only network-design calculation of the kind used in photogrammetric
network design [18] will over-value additional cameras on a rig like this by
roughly a factor of two, because it assumes that the errors it is averaging
are independent. The saturation point should be measured on the instrument,
not predicted from geometry.

\subsection{Non-uniform placement of instants has almost no headroom}
\label{sec:nonuniform}
For a smooth curve the deficit of one segment is approximately
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{i} \approx \frac{(\Delta t_{i})^{3}}{24}\,
\kappa_{i}^{2}\,\lVert \mathbf{v}_{i} \rVert^{3},
\label{eq:deficit}
\end{equation}
with $\kappa$ the curvature and $\mathbf{v}$ the velocity. Minimising
$\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}$ subject to $\sum_{i}\Delta t_{i} = T$ gives
$\Delta t_{i} \propto (\kappa_{i}^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}_{i}\rVert^{3})^{-1/3}$,
that is, instants placed at equal increments of the cumulative density
\begin{equation}
\rho(t) = \left(\kappa^{2}\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert^{3}\right)^{1/3}
= \frac{\lVert \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{a}\rVert^{2/3}}
{\lVert \mathbf{v}\rVert}.
\label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
Unlike a telemetry collar, a camera system can actually execute such a
placement, so this is the natural next lever after the budget split.

We tested three arms at identical cost: uniform placement; a deployable arm
that spends half the budget on a uniform probe, fits the state model, computes
$\rho$ from the smoothed trajectory and places the remaining instants at equal
increments of the cumulative $\rho$ while keeping the probe instants; and an
oracle arm that computes $\rho$ from the reference trajectory. The oracle is
not a deployable method but an upper bound on what any placement rule driven
by this criterion could reach. Figure~\ref{fig:nonuniform} and
table~\ref{tab:nonuniform} give the result.

The deployable rule loses everywhere, by 30\% to 170\%. Two mechanisms are
visible: equation~\eqref{eq:deficit} holds only for small $\Delta t$, so
concentrating instants in high-curvature regions creates long gaps whose cubic
penalty outweighs the local gain; and the curvature estimated from a
half-budget probe is very noisy.

The consequential number is the oracle. Even with access to the reference
trajectory, curvature-driven non-uniform placement improves on uniform
sampling by only 8\% to 25\%, with a median of 17\% across the tested cells.
Uniform sampling is close to optimal for this measurand, and an adaptive or
learned instant-selection rule has correspondingly little to win.

An earlier enumeration on the same data found a larger apparent headroom,
about 35\%, by selecting the subset of instants that directly minimises the
final path error. That criterion can exploit cancellation between over- and
under-estimates within a window, and the gap between 35\% and 17\% is a
reasonable estimate of how much of that apparent headroom is cancellation
rather than recoverable signal.

\subsection{A calibrated continuous-time estimator does not remove the effect}
\label{sec:ctsd}
A natural objection is that the corrective estimator of [4] should be used
instead of characterising the allocation. Under our state model that estimator
is the integral of the conditional expected speed. We calibrated the
observation noise from a dense consecutive burst at the native frame interval,
where measurement noise dominates process noise, obtaining 0.20 to 0.43\,mm
per record and subset.

Calibrating the noise gives a small consistent improvement to the state-mean
estimator, from 8.81 to 7.86\,mm at three views and 33 instants. The
conditional-speed estimator does reduce sensitivity to sampling density at the
sparse end, as designed: at seven views and eight instants it gives 165.2\,mm
against 242.9\,mm. But it carries a large positive bias at every setting
tested, $+42$ to $+180$\,mm, and is dominated in absolute error by the
conditional-mean-path estimator throughout. We attribute this to model
misspecification rather than to the method: [4] uses an
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process with a fitted velocity autocorrelation time and
selects the model by a corrected information criterion, whereas our
Wiener-velocity model has none. We therefore do not claim that the method of
[4] fails, only that with our state model it does not remove the allocation
effect: even granting the sparse configuration its own best estimator, the
best dense-few-view configuration remains roughly nine times more accurate at
equal cost.

\subsection{The dense-regime rise is outlier exposure, not noise accumulation}
\label{sec:outliers}
The mean absolute error over our windows rises at high sampling density, which
invites the interpretation that the classical noise-inflation limb has been
reached. The distribution, in table~\ref{tab:outliers} and
figure~\ref{fig:regimes}(a), shows otherwise.

The rise in the mean at 50 and at 200 instants is produced by a single window
each, in which one instant received a grossly wrong triangulation. This is
outlier exposure: denser sampling draws more instants and has more
opportunities to include a bad one. An order-of-magnitude estimate of the
classical mechanism for this rig, with calibrated white noise near 0.4\,mm and
about 34\,mm of motion per 20\,ms step, gives roughly 2\,mm of inflation at
the densest setting, consistent with the median flattening near 5 to 6\,mm
rather than rising.

The remedy is therefore robustness, not a change of allocation. A chi-square
gate on the Kalman innovations reduces the median error across 35 triples from
38\,mm to 6\,mm at 200 instants and improves 34 of them, precisely by removing
these windows. The same gate slightly hurts at 33 instants and below, where it
begins rejecting real fast motion, so it belongs in the dense regime only.

We flag this as a reporting caution of general relevance: the mean absolute
error alone cannot distinguish a smooth noise-accumulation mechanism from rare
gross failures, and the two call for different engineering responses.

\subsection{The opposite regime: chained single-camera egomotion}
\label{sec:opposite}
In the secondary chain the positions come from chained pairwise rigid
registrations of a single moving camera. Each edge contributes registration
noise that does not shrink with more edges, while the roughly 0.8\,m of smooth
motion in a 4\,s window is nearly fully captured by eight instants.
Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}(b) and table~\ref{tab:opposite} give the result on 14

transferred between instruments without measuring it.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Two instruments, each a single rig.} The saturation point of
about three views is a property of these camera geometries and of their
detection-error structure, not a universal constant.
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} gives the reason it cannot be universal: it
depends on how much of the error is common-mode, which is a property of the
calibration and of the target.
\item \textit{Published detections, not raw pixels.} For the multi-camera rig
we use the publisher's released centroids, so detection cost and detection
failure modes are inherited rather than measured.
\item \textit{The cost model counts processing, not acquisition.} A
capture-design reading, namely to buy three cameras rather than seven, is not
supported, because a rig built with three cameras would have different
occlusion behaviour.
\item \textit{Reference resolution.} Differences of one to three millimetres
are within the sensitivity of the reference polyline to its own sampling.
\item \textit{One target, free-space motion.} No occlusion-heavy, multi-target
or articulated cases are covered.
\item \textit{No learned component.} The oracle bound in
section~\ref{sec:nonuniform} constrains only placement rules driven by the
curvature criterion on this measurand.
\end{itemize}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
For trajectory-length measurement from a synchronised multi-camera rig under a
fixed frame budget, the budget should buy time instants rather than
simultaneous views, down to the point where view count saturates, which on our
rig is three. This gives the same accuracy at 43\% of the processed frames and
was confirmed on a pre-registered held-out set with sealed predictions. The
saturation is not the diminishing return that a geometric calculation
predicts: it occurs about twice as early because the reconstruction errors are
largely common-mode, and beyond it additional views are sometimes actively
harmful, so the saturation point should be measured on the instrument rather
than derived from network geometry. Beyond the budget split there is little
left to tune, since non-uniform placement of the instants is bounded at 8\% to
25\% even with oracle access to the reference, and the dense-sampling
degradation in our data is gross-outlier exposure best handled by a
consistency gate. A single-camera chained-registration system has the opposite
optimum, so the allocation is a property of the instrument and must be
measured for each one.
